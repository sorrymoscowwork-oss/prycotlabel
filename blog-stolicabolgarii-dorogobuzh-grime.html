Stolicabolgarii — Dorogobuzh Grime: Six Songs Outside the Rules

11.10.2026 / MUSIC REVIEW / ANTON GVITSEVICH

Some album titles explain the music; others seem to warn you not to expect an explanation. Stolicabolgarii’s Dorogobuzh Grime belongs to the second category. Dorogobuzh is a small town in Russia’s Smolensk region. Grime is a distinctly British form of electronic music that emerged from London’s underground in the early 2000s. Put the two together and you get a genre that sounds as if someone invented it late at night and decided, in the morning, not to take it back.

The joke is that there is no grime on this record. No familiar rhythmic architecture, no attempt to reproduce the genre’s recognisable sound. The word works as a deliberately misplaced label, a small provocation before the first track even begins. Think of writing “cucumber” on a chocolate bar. The mismatch is the point.

Behind the name stolicabolgarii is Sofia, a musician from Dorogobuzh and a professional theatre director and arts practitioner. She began her musical career at 34. Now living in the Middle East, she has built a recording setup at home and made this six-track EP there. It is an unusual route into music, though hardly an unheard-of one. What makes the record interesting is that it does not sound like a beginner trying to learn the rules. It sounds more like someone arriving with a different set of rules already in mind — and little interest in pretending otherwise.

Dorogobuzh Grime barely feels like a debut. There are technical rough edges, but the work itself is too self-assured to sound like a tentative first step. It feels closer to a departure from something that came before, even if we cannot quite know what that something was.

Academic instincts, broken beats

Sofia plays keyboards and has a clear affection for classical music. You do not hear this mainly through direct quotations or pastiche, but in the way the compositions think. A melody does not always serve the song; a harmony need not be comfortable; a vocal line can suddenly leave the familiar territory of pop. This is not an abstract critical claim made for the sake of sounding clever. The music itself keeps pointing in that direction.

Elsewhere, the coordinates are quite different: trip-hop, breakbeat, alternative pop and contemporary vocal experimentation. These elements collide without making much effort to reach a compromise. The EP does not so much blend genres as bring competing ideas of what a song should be into the same room.

Echoes of the classical tradition appear in arrangements whose dramatic logic keeps breaking apart. An expected development becomes a jump; a resolution gives way to a new vocal gesture. The production never settles into the smoothness that alternative pop often uses to make unusual ideas easier to digest. Some elements seem to have been left deliberately out of place. The result is a record that is not especially good at being restrained — and may not want to be.

At a private listening, the album was described as lacking balance and restraint, with a streak of naïve grotesque. In another context, that might sound like a verdict. Here it is also a fairly accurate description of the method. Contemporary art rarely depends on neat proportions alone. It can make a form expressive by exaggerating it, distorting it or pushing it slightly beyond its limits. In Sofia’s music, that grotesque quality is not always fully controlled, but it remains alive from the first track to the last.

Simple stories, an unruly form

For all its experimentation, the EP is not an intellectual puzzle. Its subjects are familiar enough: love, the effort to understand oneself and other people, events that leave a mark, places and the possibility of a future. There is no elaborate concept that the listener must decipher before the songs can mean anything. These are personal stories and reflections placed inside an unusually restless musical frame.

That distinction matters. Experimental music can become more interesting to discuss than to listen to — a fate that has befallen records with far more established reputations. Dorogobuzh Grime approaches that line at times, but it does not entirely cross it. Behind the fractured rhythms, classical gestures and strange arrangements there is a person trying to say something about her own experience. The music is more complicated than the thought it carries, and that is not necessarily a flaw.

The songs were written, sung, recorded and mixed the ordinary way: with bodies, instruments, decisions and engineering. No shortcuts. It feels almost strange to have to say this at a time when so much music arrives already packaged as content. But there is a human scale to this record, even when its arrangements threaten to overwhelm it.

From Dorogobuzh to the Middle East

Sofia lives in the Middle East, yet the EP makes little effort to turn that fact into an exotic subject. These are not songs about learning the habits of a new country or cataloguing the details of an unfamiliar life. They sound more like the work of someone who has moved somewhere else and carried the same private questions with her.

In that sense, the record may be more shaped by migration than a direct account of relocation would be. Dorogobuzh becomes a point of origin rather than a nostalgic postcard. The present is elsewhere, but the past has not simply disappeared. The distance between them gives the album part of its tension. There is a local identity without folklore, an emigrant’s perspective without the expected emigrant aesthetic, and a classical sensibility without classical conservatism. There is also pop music that keeps trying to escape the boundaries of pop.

A record that could live in an old magazine

One of the EP’s stranger qualities is visual. It is easy to imagine its world inside an old book, a theatre programme or a teenage magazine — not as retro styling, but as something between an illustration, a play and a musical fantasy. There is a certain adolescent directness here. Not childishness, but a lack of fear about seeming too serious, too strange or too emotional.

Many artists use a debut to prove they are already professionals. They polish the sound, choose their references carefully and tone down anything that might seem excessive. Stolicabolgarii does almost the opposite: she leaves the excess close to the listener. At times, the record resembles a fantasy in which Erykah Badu has wandered into a theatre workshop where the drums have been replaced by metal buckets and the guitars by hunting horns. It is not a literal comparison of sounds, but a way of describing the clash between soulful or alternative-pop feeling, theatricality, classical influence and deliberately broken rhythm. Much of the album’s identity lives in that clash.

The flaws are part of the picture — but not all of them

The sound is the most obvious point of contention. There is none of the studio polish that has become almost mandatory for a professional release. Some choices are questionable; some arrangements feel unbalanced; some vocal passages are excessive and dark. That does not automatically mean they should be fixed. Remove the weight, even out the dynamics, make the vocal more predictable and rationalise the arrangements, and the record might lose the very reason it is worth discussing — particularly on “Chudishche” and “Shanghai.”

Still, not every rough edge can be defended as an artistic decision. The line between purposeful imperfection and weak production is thin, and this EP occasionally steps across it. Sofia’s next challenge will be to learn how to control her own chaos without removing its character. The album has traces of artists as disparate as Dolphin, Aigel and South Africa’s Die Antwoord; it does not settle comfortably under any one of those names. It is as if the artist has managed to organise a chaos of thoughts into a system, without yet building a fully coherent musical framework.

Not a new genre, but a new context

It would be premature to call stolicabolgarii the inventor of a new genre. Contemporary is an established term across art and music, and the combination of alternative pop, trip-hop, breakbeat, experimental vocals and electronic treatments of classical ideas is not new in itself. But novelty does not always come from inventing new materials. Sometimes it comes from putting familiar ones into a context that changes how they sound.

On the Russian-language alternative scene, this mixture feels unusual — at times almost ostentatiously ambitious. That ambition is not necessarily a weakness. Sofia does not seem interested in lowering the scale of her ideas to make the music more convenient or easier to categorise. Some tracks are too theatrical, some vocal lines too much, and some rhythms not quite assembled. Yet it is hard to argue that there is nothing behind these decisions. There is an author here: awkward, hurried, restless, windy, but recognisable. An artistic voice tends to arrive before perfect craft.

A debut that feels like a departure

The six tracks revolve around love, self-understanding, memory and the future, but the EP feels less like an opening than a transition. As if someone were closing a previous chapter rather than turning to a blank page. Sofia came to music at 34, carrying years of experience in theatre and the arts. That background cannot simply be put aside. The result does not sound like a person who has just discovered music, but like someone who has decided to use it to express things she previously approached through other forms.

That may explain the scale of the debut. It can be uneven, loud in its artistic claims and irritating in its refusal to hold back. But it does not feel like preliminary material. It sounds like a voice — still quiet, perhaps, but confident enough to take up space.

What comes next

The project has a French-Canadian label and producer, plans for another album in 2027, and concert ambitions in Asia and Europe. The next release will show whether Dorogobuzh Grime was a fruitful experiment or the beginning of a genuinely personal artistic language.

A first record has the right to be imperfect. Sometimes it is precisely that imperfection that makes it meaningful to the artist later: a moment before the constraints have fully formed, when a musical language is still discovering its edges. This is not Bowie, and the comparison would be pointless. But Sofia’s music shares one useful quality with the artists who matter: it is not always comfortable, rational or balanced. Sometimes it is too theatrical, sometimes too naïve, sometimes too certain of its own novelty.

In an independent music culture that often learns to look professional before it has managed to become personal, that excess has value. Dorogobuzh Grime does not offer a new genre. It offers a way into someone else’s inner world. There is a good deal of imprecision and adolescent mischief behind the door. For now, that seems to be exactly where stolicabolgarii intends to build her space for expression.

I listened to the full record twice, from beginning to end, and saw early materials for the forthcoming video, photographs and a few short media sketches. If I come across a poster for a show in France, I will keep that evening free. Thank you for the trust.

Anton Gvitsevich
